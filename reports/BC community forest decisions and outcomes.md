# Real BC forests already played Cedar Bend's seven years

BC community forests have faced, in real life, almost every dilemma the Cedar Bend game asks students to decide, and the record mostly supports the game's core lesson: **community-owned forest businesses that pay costs first, bank a reserve, and then send profit back to the town do deliver visible local benefits.** Clinton's forest bought a hockey club a Zamboni. Powell River's has paid record dividends of up to about $6.3 million a year into a city reserve. Wells Gray's dividends paid to start up Clearwater's wildfire crew. And Logan Lake, after almost two decades of fuel work above town, came through a 63,548-hectare fire in 2021 without losing a single structure. The record also shows the limits a believable game should keep. Profits swing hard with lumber prices: Sechelt went from about $1.2 million in net income to a loss in one year, and Kaslo dropped a fixed payout rule because it broke in low-price years. Mill closures are bigger than anything a 20,000 m³ forest can fix. The worst real failures were governance breakdowns, not the Province cancelling a licence: no case of a cancelled or insolvent community forest turned up. For a 50–75 minute board game with about 15 students, the strongest evidence-based engagement levers are cheap ones: a prediction before every meter reveal, a short discussion right after it, a genuinely tempting "wrong" option, and a "this really happened" card after each vote that ties the room's choice to a real BC town. **One warning applies to everything below.** The research agents could mostly read only search-result snippets, not the source pages themselves, so every figure must be checked against its source before it goes on a printed card.

> **Read before printing anything.** The research agents' network access blocked most full-page fetches (bccfa.ca, gov.bc.ca, coastreporter.net, revelstokemountaineer.com, treefrogcreative.ca, therockymountaingoat.com, curriculum.gov.bc.ca and others). **Nearly every fact here comes from search-engine snippets of the cited page, not from reading the page itself.** Snippets can truncate dates, merge two stories or drop context. "High" confidence below means the snippet is specific and comes from a named outlet, a government release or a primary document such as AGM minutes, or that several sources agree. It does **not** mean a person has read the original. The list of facts to check before printing is near the end.

## Profit goes to legacy funds and small grants, after costs and reserves

The money flows through the same few steps in most towns. The forest company pays a dividend to its municipal owner. The money lands in a dedicated legacy or reserve fund rather than the general budget. A committee recommends grants, and council approves them. Sechelt's Sunshine Coast Community Forest sends every dividend to its Legacy Fund, where a committee reviews applications for council ([Coast Reporter](https://www.coastreporter.net/local-news/five-groups-receive-sccf-legacy-funds-4236528)). Nakusp's council distributes its fund under **Legacy Fund Bylaw #653**, which rules out routine village operations ([BCCFA PDF](https://www.bccfa.ca/_files/ugd/b776e1_bc8237ebda964079902097aee7b33e95.pdf)). Revelstoke's corporation pays dividends only from "excess cash not required for immediate operations" ([BCCFA](https://bccfa.ca/?p=3721)). That pipeline is itself the social-enterprise lesson. The profit is real, but it is fenced off for community purposes by rules the community wrote.

What the money buys is small, specific and easy for teenagers to picture. In July 2020 the **Clinton & District Community Forest** distributed more than $250,000: **$169,832 to the Village** (set aside for a public works yard) and **$87,075 to 11 groups**, the largest being **$40,000 for a new Zamboni** for the youth hockey club, plus $10,000 for rodeo bleachers and $2,500 each for two pit toilets ([BCCFA](https://bccfa.ca/clinton-community-forest-disburses-grants-of-more-than-250000); [MyCariboo Now](https://mycariboonow.com/?p=64468)). Sechelt's 2022 round gave **$144,000 to seven groups**, including a hall kitchen, seniors' centre audio-visual gear and a salmon hatchery's water intake, and the fund had passed **$3.9 million** in grants since 2014 ([Coast Reporter](https://www.coastreporter.net/local-news/seven-local-groups-benefit-from-community-forest-legacy-fund-grants-5755638)). Powell River's grants from its 2023 dividend included up to **$100,000 for a gardens project** and **$36,796.54 for a climbing-holds cleaning system** at a climbing co-op ([Coast Reporter](https://coastreporter.net/highlights/powell-river-community-forest-grants-handed-out-9128256)). Smithers and Telkwa's **Wetzin'kwa** corporation had funded **167 organizations** and given about **$3.9 million over 16 years** as of October 2025, including a college food bank and breakfast club ([Coast Mountain College](https://coastmountaincollege.ca/news/wetzin-kwa-community-forest-corporation-supports-cmtn-food-bank-and-breakfast-club-programs)). Revelstoke's legacy fund helped pay for a splash park ([Revelstoke Mountaineer](https://revelstokemountaineer.com/?p=38097)). Grants mostly run from $2,500 to $100,000. Big infrastructure is usually co-funded: Nakusp's arena roof drew $350,000 from Columbia Basin Trust, not the community forest ([Arrow Lakes News](https://www.arrowlakesnews.com/news/village-granted-350000-to-fix-arena-roof-4575977)).

The scale ranges widely, which suits a game that wants big moments. **Powell River** paid a record of more than $2.8 million in May 2020 and declared $3,825,934 for 2022 ([BCCFA](https://bccfa.ca/powell-river-community-forest-has-year-of-windfall-profits)). It then paid a record **$6,264,534** from 2023 before falling to about $3.7 million for 2024 ([Powell River Peak](https://www.prpeak.com/local-news/powell-river-community-forest-expenditure-approved-10912332)). **Burns Lake** split one year's profit (2016–17) equally among the Village and two First Nations, at **$412,054.92 each** ([BCCFA presentation](https://bccfa.ca/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/Burns-Lake-Frank-Varga.pdf)). In the 2020–21 lumber boom the same three partners got **$1,556,074 each** ([Lakes District News](https://www.burnslakelakesdistrictnews.com/news/burns-lake-comfor-reports-strong-fiscal-year-6309850)). A forest the size of Cedar Bend's (20,000 m³/yr) sits at the modest end. Nakusp's paid its village **$250,000 in 2025** ([NACFOR](https://www.nakuspcommunityforest.com/2025-dividend-to-village-of-nakusp/)), and its cumulative contributions were reported at **$2,344,435 since 2014** ([Penticton Herald](https://www.pentictonherald.ca/spare_news/article_ff183102-2885-5f6c-8fe9-7bf0ce439ae3.html)).

The decisions boards actually argue over are about order and timing, and the game's "Where the Profit Goes" year already models them well. Williams Lake's first cheques in 2018 combined a dividend with **repayment of start-up loans** ([BCCFA](https://bccfa.ca/williams-lake-community-forest-shareholders-reap-first-profits)). Its partners then got $987,000 (City) and $861,000 (Williams Lake First Nation) ([Williams Lake Tribune](https://wltribune.com/news/williams-lake-community-forest-shareholders-reap-first-profits)), and it sends **5% of annual profit** to a community grant fund ([BC Gov News](https://news.gov.bc.ca/releases/2023FOR0036-000925)). Kaslo's 2024 AGM scrapped a fixed **$2/m³** community-dividend levy as "unrealistic in times of low log prices." From then on, any dividend depends on a profitable year and full reserves ([KDCFS 2024 AGM minutes](https://kaslocommunityforest.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/2024-AGM-Minutes.pdf)). Sechelt's forest posted a loss for 2019 after a harvest delay and paid no extraordinary dividend, while grants continued from money already banked ([Coast Reporter](https://www.coastreporter.net/local-news/community-forest-posts-loss-hopes-to-resume-logging-in-fall-3415944)). That is the closest documented case of a reserve carrying a forest through a bad year. The research found no real case with dollar figures of a reserve "saving" a forest. The game's reserve, which pays back exactly in Fire Season, is a fair stylization, not a recreated event.

Jobs are the other half of the benefit story. BCCFA's Indicators surveys found community forests created **0.48 full-time local jobs per 1,000 m³, about 76% above the industry average** (2021/22 survey of 33 forests) ([BCCFA Indicators 2022](https://bccfa.ca/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/BCCFA-Indicators-2022-final-web-1.pdf)), up from 63% in 2018 ([BCCFA Indicators 2018](https://bccfa.ca/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/BCCFA-Indicators-2018-Sept-12-web.pdf)). Two honest cautions belong in the debrief. These are surveys run by the forests' own association, and a 2021 release claiming "more than twice as many jobs" is not directly comparable with the 76% figure ([BCCFA 2021 release](https://bccfa.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/2021-BCCFA-Press-Release-Indicators-Report.pdf)). No case of a community forest supplying land, timber or money to affordable housing was found, so a housing card should not be presented as "this really happened."

## Fire work pays off over decades, and luck still matters

**Logan Lake** is the single best card in the whole set. The District began removing beetle-killed pine above town in **2004** (180 ha) ([LLCF](https://www.llcf.ca/wildfire-risk-reduction)). By 2013 it had completed **21 fuel treatment projects on 87.4 ha** of Crown land and was a recognized FireSmart community ([BC Gov News](https://news.gov.bc.ca/releases/2013FLNR0228-001765)). By 2018 its community forest had a 10-year wildfire risk plan covering its whole tenure ([BC Gov News](https://news.gov.bc.ca/releases/2019FLNR0169-001239)). In August 2021 the Tremont Creek fire grew to **63,548 ha**, and the town of about 2,000 lost **no structures** ([ICLR](https://issuu.com/iclr/docs/cat_tales_julyaug_2021/7); [Castanet](https://www.castanetkamloops.net/news/Kamloops/476542/Kamloops-filmmaker-releasing-doc-on-Logan-Lake-s-FireSmart-success-in-face-of-2021-Tremont-Creek-fire)). A BC Wildfire Service and FPInnovations case study found crews used the treated areas on three sides of town to anchor retardant and a burnout ([FPInnovations/BCWS PDF](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/public-safety-and-emergency-services/wildfire-status/prevention/fire-fuel-management/fuels-management/wildfire-fuel_treatment_encounters_-_assessing_fuel_treatment_effectiveness_logan_lake_bc_20230403.pdf)). The mayor credited about 18 years of FireSmart work, plus **$45 home sprinklers** that bought "that little tiny bit of extra time" ([Business in Vancouver](https://www.biv.com/news/environment/45-sprinklers-helped-save-bc-town-wildfire-says-mayor-8268348)). The forest also hires high-school students as a paid **Youth FireSmart Team** ([BCCFA](https://bccfa.ca/?p=10919)), which gives students a peer to identify with.

Two details from the same record match the game's Fire Season rules closely. Logan Lake's community forest had planned a fuel treatment for summer 2021, but **the fire moved into the proposed blocks before work began** ([CFJC](https://cfjctoday.com/2021/09/23/logan-lake-hoping-to-step-up-fire-mitigation-efforts-with-funding-from-forest-enhancement-society-of-bc/)). That is a real version of "too late to thin" and of the rule closing the controlled burn at slider 80. Near Clearwater, the 2023 fire came close to the Wells Gray Community Forest, and BC Wildfire Service told a November 2023 roundtable the town **"got lucky"**: no east winds that day and no big lightning storms ([Fraser Basin Council summary](https://fraserbasin.bc.ca/_Library/Community-CWRs/cwr_clearwater_mtg_summary_nov_2_2023.pdf)). The game reads its fire outcome straight off the slider with no dice. That is a defensible teaching choice, but the chair should say aloud that real outcomes depend on wind and luck as well as preparation. Logan Lake itself was multi-causal: FireSmart work, sprinklers, suppression crews and weather all played a part.

The full range of outcomes is real, including loss. In 2018 wildfires burned about **75% of the Cheslatta Community Forest**, held by Cheslatta Carrier Nation since 2002. Much of it became uneconomic to harvest, and the Nation then won a **$1.25 million** Forest Enhancement Society grant to rehabilitate it ([The Tyee](https://thetyee.ca/News/2020/07/27/First-Nation-Built-Forestry-Future-From-Ashes-Past/); [MyPrinceGeorgeNow](https://www.myprincegeorgenow.com/125428/forest-enhancement-society-of-bc-provides-grant-to-cheslatta-carrier-nation/)). Fuel work is mostly paid for with outside grants but done by local crews. Kaslo's January 2025 project treated **10 ha for about $100,000** ([Castanet](https://www.castanetkamloops.net/news/Nelson/530753/Kaslo-Community-Forest-completes-wildfire-risk-reduction-project)). Across BC, community forests treated more than 8,000 ha before 2023 with **$8M+ of their own money and $17M+ in grants** ([BCCFA 2024 report](https://bccfa.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/BCCFA-Enhancing-Wildfire-Resilience-Report.pdf)). The clearest link to the profit year is **Wells Gray**. Clearwater paid up to **$294,532** in start-up costs for its new wildfire initial attack crew out of the community forest's dividend reserve ([B100](https://www.b100.ca/?p=114733)), which is almost exactly the game's "Hire a FireSmart crew" option.

Prescribed and cultural burning is real and partnership-led, which supports the game's link between partnership and burning. The **Williams Lake Community Forest**, co-owned by the City and Williams Lake First Nation, has run repeated burns with BC Wildfire Service: about **40 ha in 2024** and **29 ha in 2025** ([BCWS](https://blog.gov.bc.ca/bcwildfire/prescribed-burn-planned-for-williams-lake-community-forest-2/)). Williams Lake First Nation also planned a **100 ha cultural burn** on its reserve land for April 2025 ([BCWS](https://blog.gov.bc.ca/bcwildfire/cultural-burn-planned-within-williams-lake-first-nation-2/)). Esk'etemc's own company runs burns of about 57–60 ha and keeps an Elder-appointed Firekeeper ([MyCariboo Now](https://www.mycariboonow.com/83735/news/prescribed-burn-is-planned-for-the-esketemc-community-forest); [The Wren](https://thewrennews.ca/documentary-sparks-conversation-about-indigenous-led-wildfire-resilience)). Real burns run about 30–100 ha, need a cool weather window, and often follow thinning on the same block, which matches the game's "burn in the shoulder season, years ahead" logic. No measured data were found on how these burned blocks later behaved in a wildfire.

Water is the oldest stewardship story, and it is exactly the Windfall's salvage dilemma. **Harrop-Procter's** forest exists because residents formed a watershed protection committee at a public meeting on **September 10, 1984**. They moved from "stop all logging" to seeking their own tenure, won a pilot agreement in 1999 for about 11,300 ha, and still put drinking water first ([UBC case](https://cases.open.ubc.ca/?p=21010); [BCCFA profile](https://bccfa.ca/wp-content/uploads/HP.pdf)). On the Sunshine Coast, logging resumed in the **Chapman Creek** drinking watershed in 2007 after a 15-year pause and set off road blockades. In March 2008, after representations from the shíshálh Nation, the community forest was removed from the Chapman and Gray watersheds. Its current plan commits to **no harvesting in the Chapman Creek watershed for 25 years** ([SCCA](https://thescca.ca/?p=5914); [Coast Reporter](https://www.coastreporter.net/local-news/spacing-in-chapman-creek-watershed-causes-concern-3376401)). The game's salvage carry-forward, with runoff fouling the town's water, is a design invention rather than a recorded event. The real record shows towns treating a road into the drinking watershed as close to a red line.

## Recreation, partnership and conflict reward boards that share control

When the community holds the tenure, trails get designed into the harvest. When an outsider holds it, residents have to fight. **Wetzin'kwa** runs a 50+ km multi-use trail network inside a working forest ([Tourism Smithers](https://tourismsmithers.com/images/uploads/smithers-summer-trails-guide-web.pdf)). In 2024 it planned a **partial harvest, not a clearcut**, in an area that holds both a goshawk territory and ski and hiking trails ([BVRC DataHub](https://datahub.bvcentre.ca/group/wetzinwa-community-forest?tags=2023)). Kaslo's board notes show heavier cutting moved upslope and a strip "reserved for visuals from the highway" ([KDCFS Dec 2025](https://kaslocommunityforest.org/december-2025-general-board-meeting-update/)). After a winter 2025–26 harvest, its crews were "rehabilitating trails throughout the block" ([KDCFS Apr–May 2026](https://kaslocommunityforest.org/april-may-2026-general-board-meeting-update/)). The contrast is Revelstoke's Mount Macpherson, where the cutblocks belong to **BC Timber Sales, not the community forest**. Mountain bikers rallied, a petition circulated, and the City and regional district both asked the forests minister to step in. BCTS eventually added trail buffers and scaled back its plans in 2025 ([Revelstoke Mountaineer](https://revelstokemountaineer.com/revelstoke-mountain-bikers-rally-against-logging/); [Boundary Creek Times](https://www.boundarycreektimes.com/local-news/bc-timber-sales-scales-back-logging-planned-for-revelstoke-mountain-8185399)). Residents even proposed making the mountain a community forest ([Revelstoke Review](https://www.revelstokereview.com/news/disappointment-follows-revelstoke-forestry-open-house/)). The Macpherson story should be told as "a town wanted a community forest because it had no say," never as "the community forest logged the trails." Trail users are not one bloc either: the Nordic ski club reportedly backs some forest-health logging. The Trail Network year's economics have a real anchor: Golden's 2023 study counted just under **7,400 mountain bikers spending about $5.8 million** in a summer ([GCC report](https://goldencyclingclub.com/cms/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Summer-2023-Golden-Mountain-Biking-Economic-Impact-Final-Report-updated-May.pdf)). No signed agreement between a community forest and a trail club was verified.

First Nations partnerships are the most common and best-documented success pattern, which supports the game's positive framing of the Partnership year. **33 of BC's 62 community forest agreements** are held by or with First Nations ([BCCFA](https://www.bccfa.ca/community-forestry)). **Cheakamus** near Whistler has been run since 2009 by the Squamish Nation, Lil'wat Nation and the Resort Municipality of Whistler as three equal partners ([Forest Trends](https://www.forest-trends.org/articles/how-two-first-nations-and-a-small-canadian-town-tapped-carbon-finance-to-better-manage-their-shared-forest/)). The partnership changed the forest. Faced with one faction wanting a working forest and another wanting "a fence around it," the partners compromised on ecosystem-based management: 1–5 ha cutblocks, wider buffers, and an annual cut of **21,000 m³ instead of a possible 41,170 m³** ([BC Gov News](https://news.gov.bc.ca/releases/2015FLNR0075-000574)). Carbon offsets paid for much of the difference. The **Williams Lake** forest, a 50/50 partnership of the City and Williams Lake First Nation with three directors from each, won the province's **2023 Robin Hood Memorial Award**. Judges cited the relationship between the partners and its fuel-reduction program ([BC Gov News](https://news.gov.bc.ca/releases/2023FOR0036-000925)). **Burns Lake** gives three of six board seats to First Nations representatives and splits dividends equally. Evidence on measured cultural outcomes, such as protected sites or the use of place names, was not found for any partnership. The cards should therefore stick to governance and money facts and avoid speaking for any Nation.

The honest failures are governance failures, and they belong in the game as warnings, not as the main story. Around November 2018 the Sunshine Coast forest went ahead with logging **14 ha of lower Mount Elphinstone** after a public rally. The chair said: "We said we would listen to the community, and we did listen" ([Coast Reporter](https://www.coastreporter.net/local-news/logging-plans-unchanged-opposition-kicks-into-gear-3374897)). Protesters were arrested, including a 79-year-old shíshálh elder ([AMMSA](https://ammsa.com/publications/ravens-eye/sechelt-elder-arrested-sunshine-coast-logging-protesters)). The chair, the vice-chair and another director later resigned amid a court challenge over consultation ([Coast Reporter](https://www.coastreporter.net/local-news/changes-coming-as-community-forest-directors-resign-3407505)). The nearby Clack Creek blockade of 2019–20 was a BC Timber Sales sale, not a community forest decision, and the two are often confused ([The Narwhal](https://thenarwhal.ca/weve-been-cheated-sunshine-coast-community-braces-for-logging-of-forest-at-heart-of-park-proposal/)). **McBride's** community forest ran a **$215,000 deficit in 2015**. The board fired its manager and called in outside reviewers, and a resident complained that "it never should have been just mayor and council on the board" ([Rocky Mountain Goat](https://therockymountaingoat.com/2016/08/mcbride-community-forest-board-loses-members/)). The research found **no case of a BC community forest agreement being cancelled or a forest going insolvent**. The game's "Treasury hits zero, the Province takes it back" rule is a fair simplification of what is at stake, but the chair should call it a game rule. The real-world version is closer to McBride: losses, a fired manager, outside reviewers and lost public trust.

## Booms, duties and mill closures test who keeps the value

Markets drive the treasury far more than any board decision. BC lumber hit a record of about **US$1,600–1,670 per thousand board feet in spring 2021** ([Central 1](https://www.central1.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/ECON_COMMENTARY_feb-2023_forestry.pdf)). The Cascade Lower Canyon forest paid each partner **$150,000** at the end of 2021, its largest dividend ever, because "lumber prices [were] exceptionally strong" ([Hope Standard](https://www.hopestandard.com/news/district-of-hope-receives-150000-from-community-forest-project/)). Then came the squeeze. The combined US duty on most Canadian softwood rose from 8.05% to **14.54% in August 2024** ([Global Affairs Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/global-affairs/news/2024/08/statement-by-minister-ng-on-us-department-of-commerce-fifth-review-of-duties-on-canadian-softwood-lumber.html)), then to about **35% in 2025** ([Global News](https://globalnews.ca/news/11305057/softwood-lumber-tariffs-us-anti-dumping)), with a separate **10% tariff added from October 14, 2025** ([Global Affairs Canada](https://www.international.gc.ca/controls-controles/softwood-bois_oeuvre/index.aspx?view=d&lang=eng)). Community forests sell logs rather than lumber, so they feel these shocks indirectly, through lower log prices and fewer buyers. Kaslo's abandoned $2/m³ levy is the clearest sign of it.

Closures hit small towns at a scale the game's Mill year should respect. **Fraser Lake** lost about **175 mill jobs in a village of about 965** people in 2024 ([CBC](https://amp.cbc.ca/lite/story/1.7091613)). **100 Mile House** lost about 165 direct jobs in late 2025, and the mayor expected at least 500 more indirect jobs to go ([CFJC](https://cfjctoday.com/2025/11/08/100-mile-house-mayor-profoundly-disappointed-by-impending-sawmill-closure/)). The industry council estimated about **10,000 jobs lost in 2023** ([KelownaNow](https://amp.kelownanow.com/watercooler/news/news/BC_Interior/There_s_a_lot_at_stake_BC_s_forestry_industry_lost_10_000_jobs_in_2023)). The most useful case for the game is **Vavenby**. When Canfor closed its mill in 2019 (at least 172 jobs), it sold the cutting rights to Interfor, while the Simpcw First Nation and the District of Clearwater opposed the transfer. As part of the deal Canfor put **$500,000 into the Wells Gray Community Forest** plus a $200,000 legacy fund ([CBC](https://amp.cbc.ca/lite/story/1.5480900); [CFJC](https://cfjctoday.com/?p=763571)). A company's rights can be sold out of town; a community forest's tenure stays with the town. That is the Windfall buyout's lesson in real form. The Golden precedent from earlier research makes the same point from another angle: a 20,000 m³ community forest allocation made in 2006 went to BC Timber Sales in 2010 before any licence was issued ([Golden & Area CF Initiative](https://goldenareacf.com/about)). The Province's recent response has been to **expand community forests in closure-hit areas**, including Vanderhoof, Fort St. James and Fraser Lake in 2025 ([BC Gov News](https://news.gov.bc.ca/releases/2025FOR0041-000920)) and McLeod Lake Mackenzie in 2026 ([BC Gov News](https://news.gov.bc.ca/releases/2026FOR0047-001063)).

The "local makers" and carbon options are both real but small, and the game should not oversell either. Harrop-Procter's own FSC-certified mill employs about **5 full-time staff plus part-timers** ([Wood Business](https://www.woodbusiness.ca/sawmill-provides-environmental-and-economic-benefits-for-community-forest-in-bc-4777/)). Barriere's community forest runs a micro-mill and a firewood business, but it still sold about **97% of its logs to big mills**. In 2020 only **2 of 27** surveyed community forests ran their own mill ([Barriere Star Journal](https://www.barrierestarjournal.com/news/lower-north-thompson-community-forest-makes-small-mill-enterprises-work/)). The epilogue line that local makers "now employ thirty people" is generous next to these figures. Something like "a dozen" would be more defensible. On carbon, Cheakamus earns about $100,000 a year from offsets in ordinary years, and BC's 2025 pricing change brought a single purchase of more than **$600,000** from a mining company ([Business in Vancouver](https://www.biv.com/news/resources-agriculture/new-carbon-pricing-fuels-600-revenue-surge-for-bc-community-forest-12259045)). A BCCFA manager nonetheless calls the carbon route "such a dead end for us," and only a couple of BC community forests have ever sold credits in the government market ([BIV](https://www.biv.com/news/environment/experts-warn-bcs-forest-carbon-market-hitting-a-dead-end-12291061)). The game's carbon-versus-thinning clash is plausible design. No real carbon contract was found that blocked fuel treatment.

## Fifteen students need prediction, a real temptation and a true story every year

The evidence on classroom simulations is moderate, and it points to facilitation habits rather than flashy mechanics. Simulation is among the more effective game types in secondary school ([Zenodo review](https://zenodo.org/records/3276840/files/paper1.pdf)). In Mazur's physics demonstration study, students who only watched learned no more than students who never saw the demo, while those who **predicted first** learned significantly more ([Crouch et al.](https://www.otffeo.on.ca/en/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2014/11/Mazur_demo-article.pdf)). Debriefing improved learning among 238 high-school economics students, though the effects were small ([Feinberg](https://getd.libs.uga.edu/pdfs/feinberg_joseph_r_200308_phd.pdf)). Debriefing **during** play and **as a team** beat solo or end-of-game debriefs for motivation, though that study was small and used sixth graders ([Bilgin et al.](https://scholarworks.boisestate.edu/edtech_facpubs/139)). In the KEEP COOL climate game, played by more than 200 students aged 13–16, students reported more personal responsibility and more optimism afterwards. The effect was strongest among those who played damaging roles, and the authors advise letting players freely choose the harmful path rather than making the green path the only way to win ([Meya & Eisenack](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs10584-018-2254-7)). Ojala's work with high-school students found that "constructive hope," which takes the problem seriously while seeing reachable solutions, supports engagement, while hope built on denial undermines it ([NAAEE summary](https://eepro.naaee.org/eepro/research/library/hope-and-climate-change-importance-hope)). In World Climate, gains in urgency, not knowledge, predicted the intention to act ([MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/121083)). Most commons games (Fishbanks, the Fishing Game) teach through collapse ([SERC](https://serc.carleton.edu/NAGTWorkshops/complexsystems/activities/fishing_game.html)). A game about social-enterprise success therefore needs Ostrom's conditions instead: talk before deciding, and rules the group sets for itself ([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2010-04-trumps-penalties-social-ecological.pdf)). Talk alone is not enough without transparency and trust ([IASC 2025](https://2025.iasc-commons.org/?p=8190)). Facilitator skill and time for reflection decided outcomes across 20 educational board games ([ASEE 2026](https://nemo.asee.org/public/conferences/374/papers/51789/view)).

Fifteen students changes the classroom maths compared with the 30 the current `CLASSROOM-MECHANICS.md` assumes. Everyone can speak in every major year, an odd board size prevents tied votes, and the secret ballot becomes quick enough to use more often. It also means a few disengaged students are a large share of the room. The recommendations below are labelled by evidence strength. **Research-backed** means a peer-reviewed study or dissertation supports the mechanism (often in a different age group or subject). **Practitioner** means published facilitator guidance. **Inferred** means a design judgment drawn from the research or the real cases above.

## Conclusion

The research changes the game's emphasis more than its mechanics. The most persuasive real successes are not the large dividend cheques. They are **boring decisions that paid off years later**: Logan Lake cutting dead pine in 2004 and keeping every building in 2021, Kaslo refusing to pay out until its reserve was full, Williams Lake repaying its start-up loans before celebrating. That is a harder lesson for teenagers than "share the profit," and it is the one the game's reserve, burning and partnership mechanics already encode. The story cards should make students feel the delay between a choice and its payoff. Meanwhile the real failures (Sechelt's Elphinstone protests, McBride's deficit) show that the threat to a social enterprise is usually lost trust, not lost money. That argues for treating the three community meters as seriously as the Treasury in the debrief.

The engagement evidence and the case evidence point the same way. Students engage most when they predict and then see the consequence, when the selfish option is genuinely available, and when the outcome is believable. A real town's story after each vote does all three at once, for about 30 seconds of class time. The main open risk is factual, not pedagogical. Every card rests on snippets, so one evening with the source pages, starting with Logan Lake, Clinton and Wells Gray, is the difference between a credible facilitator and a corrected one.
