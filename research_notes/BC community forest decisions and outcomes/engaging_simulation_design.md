# Engaging classroom governance/economics simulation design (community forest board sim, ~15 students, ages 14-18)

Method note: Web search plus attempted fetches. Direct page fetches to ideas.repec.org, img.climateinteractive.org and curriculum.gov.bc.ca all failed (hosts outside this session's allowed network domains), so most findings below rest on search-result snippets and summaries. Confidence is marked: [R] = research-backed (peer-reviewed or a dissertation), [P] = practitioner or vendor guidance, [I] = my own inference for design, not sourced.

## 1. What drives engagement and learning in secondary-classroom simulations, and common failure modes

### Takeaway
The evidence is moderate. Simulation is among the more effective game types in secondary school, and students learn more when they act than when they watch. Debriefing helps, but the measured effect sizes are small and the studies are few. The things that make or break a session are facilitation, enough time to reflect, believability, and some autonomy. The typical ways sessions fail are boredom or confusion (which lead to students gaming the system), unclear purpose, and over-planning ("analysis paralysis").

### Cited Findings
- [R] A 2019 systematic review of game-based learning in secondary education found simulation among the most effective game types. — [Zenodo, 2019 review](https://zenodo.org/records/3276840/files/paper1.pdf)
- [R] Sitzmann's (2011) meta-analysis (adult and training settings): learners gained more from simulation games when the game presented material actively, but less than from comparison methods that were also active. In other words, the active engagement matters more than the game format. — [Karl Kapp summary of meta-analyses](https://karlkapp.com/?p=4333)
- [R] Feinberg (2003, UGA dissertation): 238 high-school economics students. Oral, written and combined debriefing all improved immediate cognitive learning compared with no debrief, but the effect sizes were small. The author notes that earlier debriefing findings were too few and too contradictory to support broad generalisations. — [Feinberg dissertation](https://getd.libs.uga.edu/pdfs/feinberg_joseph_r_200308_phd.pdf)
- [R] Bilgin, Baek & Park (2015), 62 sixth graders: team debriefing raised motivation and self-efficacy more than self-debriefing did, and debriefing during the game beat debriefing after it on those measures. — [Boise State ScholarWorks](https://scholarworks.boisestate.edu/edtech_facpubs/139)
- [R] Crouch, Fagen, Callan & Mazur (2004, Am. J. Physics): students who passively watched demonstrations understood the concepts no better than students who never saw them. Students who predicted the outcome first understood significantly more. Second-hand effect sizes: watch only about 0.15, predict then watch about 0.26, predict, watch and discuss about 0.34. These numbers were not verified against the original. — [OTF copy of Mazur article](https://www.otffeo.on.ca/en/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2014/11/Mazur_demo-article.pdf); [Glessmer summary](https://mirjamglessmer.com/2014/06/01/how-to-make-demos-useful-in-teaching/)
- [R] A 2026 ASEE literature review of 20 educational board games, including KEEP COOL, found that positive outcomes often required skilled facilitation and enough classroom time for play and reflection. — [ASEE 2026](https://nemo.asee.org/public/conferences/374/papers/51789/view)
- [R] Gaming the system (rapid guessing, exploiting mechanics) is a form of behavioural disengagement and is negatively associated with learning. Boredom and confusion tend to come before it. — summarised from [BYU ScholarsArchive](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/etd/5950) and related results (snippet-level confidence)
- [R/P] A technical-writing simulation study: the simulation must be believable to work, and making it too game-like can backfire with some students. Interest stayed higher when the simulation was complex and gave students some autonomy. — [core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/works/10870672) (snippet-level)
- [P] Business-simulation practitioners warn of "analysis paralysis" when students over-plan before playing, and recommend a practice round. — [Capsim](https://capsim.com/masterclass/boost-engagement-with-practice-rounds)
- [P] Friction and unclear purpose cause disengagement. Fixes: explain what the scoring is for, separate practice results from real results, and run a structured debrief. — [macmyths practitioner guide](https://macmyths.com/how-to-fix-low-engagement-in-a-university-marketing-game/)
- [P] Fishbanks facilitators say the debrief is "as important, if not more important, than the simulation itself." — [Telfer School, uOttawa](https://telfer.uottawa.ca/en/latest-news/innovation-and-entrepreneurship/a-simple-simulation-to-demonstrate-a-complex-topic-about-sustainable-development)

### Inferences
- [I] Ask for a prediction before each meter reveal. Prediction is the best-evidenced, lowest-cost engagement lever, and it fits a projected board sim naturally: "Before I click, where will Forest Health go: up, flat or down?"
- [I] Keep scenarios grounded in real BC community-forest cases so they stay believable, and avoid cartoonish point systems. Real-world comparison after each vote also builds credibility.
- [I] Run one short practice dilemma (2-3 minutes) to teach how voting and reveals work before the scored years.
- [I] Debrief in small doses inside the game, with 30-60 seconds of discussion after each reveal, plus a final debrief. The Bilgin finding supports in-game debriefing, though its sample was small and in a different grade.

### Gaps
- I found no meta-analysis focused only on secondary-school simulations with debrief as a variable. The 2023 scoping review in *Technology, Pedagogy and Education* is focused on secondary classrooms, but I could not read it. — [link](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/15391523.2023.2186546)
- I found no peer-reviewed work isolating the facilitator's behaviour as a variable.

## 2. Known resource-management and commons games and what makes them work

### Takeaway
The commons games that work best (Fishbanks, the Fishing Game, World Climate, KEEP COOL) share three features: visible delayed feedback from a stock-and-flow resource, real choice (including the option to play selfishly), and a strong debrief. In most of them the typical outcome is overshoot and collapse, so the learning comes from failure. For a social-enterprise-success message, the evidence from Ostrom's work and from World Climate suggests designing in communication, rule-making and a hopeful "solutions" reveal.

### Cited Findings
- [P] Fishbanks (Meadows; web version by MIT Sloan/Forio): teams run fishing companies to maximise net worth. Players "all tend to exceed the maximum size of the fleet for rational and sustainable exploitation… despite having learned about renewal dynamics." The debrief leads into Hardin's Tragedy of the Commons. — [Telfer](https://telfer.uottawa.ca/en/latest-news/innovation-and-entrepreneurship/a-simple-simulation-to-demonstrate-a-complex-topic-about-sustainable-development); [USF/IAN reports of collapse](https://ian.umces.edu/blog/one-fish-two-fish-one-fish-wait-where-did-all-the-fish-go/)
- [P] Fishbanks logistics: one full 90-minute class for play, then debrief in part or all of the next class. K-12 educators can register free as academic users. The board version ships with an intro slide deck, a debrief slide deck and a manual. A MERLOT reviewer found no stated learning objectives and little in-game instruction. — [sustainabilityscience.org instructor PDF](https://sustainabilityscience.org/course/course-library/learning-games/fishbanks-instructors.pdf); [System Dynamics Society board set](https://systemdynamics.org/product/fish-banks-ltd-board-game-set/); [MERLOT](https://www.merlot.org/merlot/viewCompositeReview.htm?id=1384340)
- [P] Note on timing: Fishbanks typically takes about 90 minutes of play plus a separate debrief, which is longer than this sim's 50-75 minute slot. This supports a tighter design. (Inference from the logistics above.)
- [P] The Fishing Game (SERC/Carleton) "almost always results in a crash of the fishing population and ultimate demise of the fishing company." — [SERC Fishing Game](https://serc.carleton.edu/NAGTWorkshops/complexsystems/activities/fishing_game.html)
- [R] Mudaliar, Koontz & Harley (IASC 2025): in the fishing game across three institutions, communication without transparency and trust was not enough to produce cooperation between groups. Students called for regulation while distrusting government. They gained understanding of communication, trust and institutions. — [IASC 2025](https://2025.iasc-commons.org/?p=8190)
- [R] Ostrom lab experiments: letting parties talk to each other improved cooperation and reduced over-harvesting. Without communication, participants over-harvest. — [phys.org summary](https://phys.org/news/2010-04-trumps-penalties-social-ecological.pdf); [MobLab teaching Ostrom (vendor)](https://moblab.com/teaching-elinor-ostrom-commons)
- [R] University of Louisville (N=186): playing a serious social-dilemma game before instruction improved conceptual understanding of the tragedy of the commons. — (reported in search summary; source URL [par.nsf.gov](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10406385), snippet-level confidence)
- [R] Ostrom's eight design principles (*Governing the Commons*, 1990) come from hundreds of cases of forests and water, and include clear boundaries and cheap conflict resolution. A student meta-project found that not all principles need to be present, and that they tend to work in clusters. — [ASU news](https://ke.news.prod.rtd.asu.edu/2016/10/07/learning-share-principles-governing-commons); [OWU course blog](https://sites.owu.edu/sustainability-workblog/2023/04/09/lli399-monday-april-10/); [Tufts Civic Studies](https://sites.tufts.edu/civicstudies/major_topics/norms-for-cooperation)
- [R] World Climate Simulation (Climate Interactive / MIT; a role-play with the C-ROADS model projected live): Rooney-Varga, Sterman et al., *PLOS ONE* 2018. Pre/post surveys of more than 2,000 participants in 39 sessions across 8 nations. Climate knowledge, affective engagement and desire to learn more all increased. Gains in urgency, not gains in knowledge, were associated with intent to act. Gains were as strong among US participants who oppose free-market regulation. The study is uncontrolled and evaluates the whole experience, not the debrief alone. — [DSpace@MIT](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/121083); [MIT Sloan](https://mitsloan.mit.edu/ideas-made-to-matter/tool-pushing-people-to-take-action-climate-change)
- [P] World Climate facilitator guide: covers preparation, room setup, sample scripts and debrief techniques, and there is a 20-minute short version. One educator asked students in the debrief to predict what adults would do in the same game. A Vermont high-school write-up said students "asked a lot of good questions and stayed engaged in the role play." — [CI facilitator resources](https://www.climateinteractive.org/programs/world-climate/facilitator-resources); [CI customisation tips](https://www.climateinteractive.org/blog/tips-on-how-to-customize-world-climate-for-different-audiences); [Vermont HS](https://climateinteractive.org/blog/high-school-students-in-vermont-experience-world-climate); guide PDF (not fetched, host blocked): [v33](https://img.climateinteractive.org/2018/04/World-Climate-Facilitator-Guide-v33.pdf); En-ROADS guides: [Workshop v35](https://img.climateinteractive.org/2025/08/En-ROADS-Workshop-Facilitator-Guide-Aug25-v35.pdf), [Game v33 Feb 2026](https://img.climateinteractive.org/2026/02/En-ROADS-Game-Facilitator-Guide-February-2026-v33.pdf)
- [R] KEEP COOL climate board game: Meya & Eisenack (*Climatic Change*, 2018), more than 200 German students aged 13-16. After playing, students reported a statistically significant rise in personal responsibility and more optimism about international climate policy. Effects were stronger for students who played climate-damaging roles. The authors' design lesson: let players choose freely between sustainable and damaging strategies, rather than making the green path the only way to win. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs10584-018-2254-7); [HU Berlin](https://edoc.hu-berlin.de/handle/18452/19316); [idw news](https://idw-online.de/en/news700277)

### Inferences
- [I] Copy World Climate's core trick: live projected model output immediately after a group decision. This sim already does that with its meters, so the design work should go into making each reveal dramatic: pause, ask for a prediction, then click.
- [I] The KEEP COOL result argues against making the "right" (social-enterprise) answer obvious. Dilemmas should have a tempting short-term-profit option that genuinely pays off on some meters, so that choosing the community or sustainable option is a real decision.
- [I] Most commons games teach through collapse. To land the success of social enterprise instead, build in the conditions Ostrom found make commons work: talking before voting, the board writing its own rules (e.g., a harvest cap it sets in year 1), and monitoring. Then show those choices paying off over several years. Delays matter: show short-term costs and long-term gains.
- [I] Catan-style resource trading, Model UN and mock-council formats were not researched in depth here. Mock-council procedure (motion, debate, vote) maps directly onto the board format.

### Gaps
- I found no formal learning-outcome study for Fishbanks itself. The evidence is anecdotal.
- No information was found on Forestia, Stop Disasters (UNDRR), or the "Marshmallow"/"Island" economics games within the search budget.
- I could not read the exact World Climate debrief questions because the guide host was blocked.

## 3. Mechanics for ~15 students playing as themselves (no assigned roles)

### Takeaway
Few studies test individual mechanics. The best-evidenced are prediction before reveal, peer discussion, and (in commons research) communication before decisions. Other mechanics (secret ballots, budget tokens, event cards, personal "shares") are practitioner or design inference. They are listed below as candidate mechanics tied to the evidence they draw on.

### Cited Findings
- [R] Prediction followed by discussion produces the largest conceptual gains of the demonstration formats tested. — [Mazur article](https://www.otffeo.on.ca/en/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2014/11/Mazur_demo-article.pdf)
- [R] Communication before harvest decisions increases cooperation in commons experiments, but communication alone is not enough without transparency and trust. — [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2010-04-trumps-penalties-social-ecological.pdf); [IASC 2025](https://2025.iasc-commons.org/?p=8190)
- [R] Team debriefing beats individual debriefing for motivation and self-efficacy (sixth graders). — [Bilgin et al. 2015](https://scholarworks.boisestate.edu/edtech_facpubs/139)
- [R] Free choice of strategy, including the damaging one, strengthened attitude change in KEEP COOL. — [Meya & Eisenack](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs10584-018-2254-7)
- [P] Separate practice from scored rounds, and explain the purpose of scoring. — [Capsim](https://capsim.com/masterclass/boost-engagement-with-practice-rounds); [macmyths](https://macmyths.com/how-to-fix-low-engagement-in-a-university-marketing-game/)

### Inferences (candidate mechanics, all [I] unless noted)
- **Predict, then reveal (every round):** before the facilitator clicks, every student shows a prediction (thumbs up, flat or down for each meter, or holds up an A/B/C card). Optionally score accurate predictions on a "foresight" leaderboard. This gives individual stakes without assigned roles.
- **Secret ballot, then reveal:** voting on paper slips or face-down cards, revealed together, reduces conformity to the loudest voice. Follow with a 60-second "minority report" in which someone from the losing side explains their vote. (No source found specifically for secondary sims; general rationale.)
- **Limited influence tokens:** each student gets about 3 tokens for the whole game to add weight to decisions they care about most. This forces priority-setting, a core board skill, and gives quiet students leverage.
- **Personal share or dividend:** each student holds one "community share". In the final year, a community-dividend meter (e.g., local jobs and grants) pays every shareholder equally, while a few dilemmas offer a private side-payment. This creates a tension like a commons dilemma while students still play as themselves.
- **"Breaking news" event cards:** unexpected shocks (wildfire, mill closure, a First Nation partnership offer, a timber price spike) add surprise and test whether earlier resilience choices pay off. Climate Interactive and Fishbanks both use exogenous shifts; the specifics here are inference.
- **Time-boxed debate:** 90 seconds for each side, then the vote. Prevents analysis paralysis ([Capsim](https://capsim.com/masterclass/boost-engagement-with-practice-rounds) supports the risk, not this exact fix).
- **"What did the real community forest do?" reveal:** after each vote, show what a real BC community forest did and what happened. This adds believability ([core.ac.uk finding](https://core.ac.uk/works/10870672)) and reframes outcomes as learning rather than winning or losing.
- **Board bylaw moment:** early in the game, the board votes on one standing rule (e.g., annual cut cap or a percentage of profits to the community). This is Ostrom-style rule-making, and later events show how the rule played out.
- **Cooperative overall goal with mild competition:** the whole board shares the forest meters (cooperative), and competition is confined to individual prediction scores. No source compares these directly for this age group.

### Gaps
- I found no studies comparing secret and open ballots, token allocation, or betting mechanics in secondary classroom sims.
- I found no specific research on group size around 15. Most commons games use teams of 3-5, so this sim could split into 3 "committees" of 5 for discussion before a whole-board vote [I].

## 4. Keeping the tone positive (learning what works) while keeping real stakes

### Takeaway
Research on hope in environmental education (Ojala) shows that "constructive hope" (seeing the seriousness of a problem plus reachable goals) supports engagement, while hope based on denial undermines it. Teachers foster it by acknowledging negative emotions and communicating in a solution-oriented, future-focused way. Kapur's productive-failure research suggests that designed, psychologically safe failure improves transfer. Together these argue for real setbacks inside a framework that is visibly recoverable and solution-focused.

### Cited Findings
- [R] Ojala (2015), Swedish high-school students: constructive hope was positively related to pro-environmental engagement, and hope based on denial was negatively related. Constructive hope was linked to teachers who respect students' negative emotions and use a future-oriented, positive, solution-oriented communication style. — [NAAEE eePRO summary](https://eepro.naaee.org/eepro/research/library/hope-and-climate-change-importance-hope); [bibbase](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/ojala-hopeinthefaceofclimatechangeassociationswithenvironmentalengagementandstudentperceptionsofteachersemotioncommunicationstyleandfutureorientation-2015)
- [R] Hope and anxiety coexist in English students, and both relate to environmental action. — [Taylor & Francis 2022](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13504622.2022.2120963)
- [R] World Climate: gains in urgency (an affective response) predicted intent to act, while knowledge gains did not, so emotional stakes matter. — [DSpace@MIT](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/121083)
- [R] KEEP COOL players became more optimistic about effective policy after play, which shows that a game can raise both responsibility and optimism. — [idw](https://idw-online.de/en/news700277)
- [R/P] Kapur, productive failure: matches direct instruction on recall and outperforms it on transfer. Students need to feel safe making mistakes. — [Imperial College blog](https://blogs.imperial.ac.uk/education-research/?p=374)

### Inferences
- [I] Make setbacks recoverable: a bad year dents the meters, but a later good decision can repair them. Avoid a single irreversible "game over".
- [I] Celebrate wins visibly with sound, colour and a "milestone unlocked" card (e.g., "Your forest funded the local school's outdoor program, just like [real community forest]"). This lets the social-enterprise successes arrive as rewards.
- [I] Frame the end as "what worked" first, then "what we'd change". This matches constructive hope: honest about trade-offs, focused on reachable solutions.
- [I] Keep stakes real through the visible meters, the personal share/dividend and prediction scores, rather than through punishment.

### Gaps
- I found no study directly comparing reward-framed and loss-framed commons games for teens.

## 5. Debrief structures that cement learning

### Takeaway
"What? So what? Now what?" (Borton 1970, later Driscoll and Rolfe et al. 2001) is the standard, simple three-stage structure. Evidence shows debriefing helps, with modest effects, and that team or in-game debriefing helps motivation. A practical design combines micro-debriefs after each round with a final structured debrief and a concept "cash-in" that names the ideas students experienced.

### Cited Findings
- [P] What? (describe what happened and how it felt), So what? (analysis and theory), Now what? (action and transfer). The simplicity of the model is a strength, but it risks shallow reflection if the questions are weak. — [Univ. of Edinburgh Reflection Toolkit](https://reflection.ed.ac.uk/reflectors-toolkit/reflecting-on-experience/what-so-what-now-what); [Hull libguides](https://libguides.hull.ac.uk/reflectivewriting/rolfe); [Earth Leadership group-debrief variant](https://earthleadership.org/post/learning-cycle-what-so-what-now-what)
- [R] Debriefing improved immediate learning for high-school economics students (small effects). Oral, written and combined formats were all tested. — [Feinberg 2003](https://getd.libs.uga.edu/pdfs/feinberg_joseph_r_200308_phd.pdf)
- [R] In-game and team debriefing beat post-game and self debriefing for motivation and self-efficacy. — [Bilgin et al. 2015](https://scholarworks.boisestate.edu/edtech_facpubs/139)
- [P] World Climate educator technique: in the debrief, ask students to predict how adults would play. — [CI tips](https://www.climateinteractive.org/blog/tips-on-how-to-customize-world-climate-for-different-audiences)
- [P] Fishbanks: the debrief connects play to Hardin's Tragedy of the Commons, and facilitators allow a full session for it. — [Telfer](https://telfer.uottawa.ca/en/latest-news/innovation-and-entrepreneurship/a-simple-simulation-to-demonstrate-a-complex-topic-about-sustainable-development)
- [R/P] One university course had student groups apply Ostrom's design principles to their own group. A similar step would work as a concept cash-in. — [OWU](https://sites.owu.edu/sustainability-workblog/2023/04/09/lli399-monday-april-10/)

### Inferences (suggested 12-15 minute final debrief, [I])
1. **What? (3 min):** reread the meter timeline on screen. Ask: "Which vote are you proudest of? Which would you change?"
2. **So what? (5 min), concept cash-in:** put 4-5 concept cards on screen (triple bottom line; social enterprise and reinvesting profit in the community; tragedy of the commons and Ostrom's principles; long-term versus short-term trade-offs; consultation and partnership with First Nations). Students match each card to a moment in their own game.
3. **Real-world comparison (3 min):** "Here is what [real BC community forest] did over 20 years." Compare their board's choices.
4. **Now what? (2-3 min):** exit ticket with one idea they would use in their own venture or community, written individually (combining oral and written debriefs, as Feinberg tested).
- Also run a micro-debrief of 30-60 seconds after each reveal: "Surprised? Why did that meter move?"

### Gaps
- I could not access the specific debrief question sets in the World Climate or Fishbanks guides (blocked or login-only).

## 6. Alignment with the BC curriculum (ADST Entrepreneurship, Social Studies, First Peoples Principles of Learning)

### Takeaway
BC's ADST Entrepreneurship and Marketing courses explicitly include social, ethical and sustainability considerations in decision-making, and the role of social entrepreneurship in First Nations communities. The First Peoples Principles of Learning (FNESC) begin with well-being of "the self, the family, the community, the land, the spirits, and the ancestors". This fits a community-forest board sim closely. The official ministry pages could not be fetched, so the wording comes from school-district copies.

### Cited Findings
- [Curriculum, secondary copy] Entrepreneurship and Marketing 10 big ideas include "Social, ethical, and sustainability considerations impact design and decision making" and "Ethical marketing contributes to a healthier global marketplace." — [SD44 course outline](https://www.sd44.ca/school/argyle/StaffInfo/StaffContactsLinks/Documents/Entrepreneurship%20and%20Marketing%2010.pdf)
- [Curriculum, secondary copy] Grade 9 Entrepreneurship and Marketing content includes "the role of social entrepreneurship in First Nations communities"; Grades 6-7 cover "characteristics of social entrepreneurship in First Nations communities." — [BC ADST Indigenous Knowledge and Perspectives K-12 (PDF)](https://curriculum.gov.bc.ca/sites/curriculum.gov.bc.ca/files/curriculum/indigenous-knowledge-and-perspectives/adst-k-12-indigenous-knowledge-and-perspectives.pdf) (as summarised in search results)
- [Curriculum] The ministry ADST document says the First Peoples Principles of Learning are reflected throughout the curriculum's rationale, goals, big ideas, competencies and elaborations, as explicit and implicit references. — [same ADST IKP doc](https://curriculum.gov.bc.ca/sites/curriculum.gov.bc.ca/files/curriculum/indigenous-knowledge-and-perspectives/adst-k-12-indigenous-knowledge-and-perspectives.pdf)
- [Curriculum, secondary copy] Marketing and Promotion 11: "Marketing services and products can be designed through consultation and collaboration." — [NVOL outline](https://onlinelearning.sd44.ca/media/nvsd/nvsd---schools/nvsd---north-vancouver-online-learning/content-assets/documents/course-outlines/Marketing-and-Promotion-11-NVOL-Course-Outline.pdf)
- [FNESC] First Peoples Principles of Learning: first principle, "Learning ultimately supports the well-being of the self, the family, the community, the land, the spirits, and the ancestors." FNESC says the principles cannot represent the practices of every Nation and are a guide. Other principles (not quoted in the results I saw, but widely known from the same poster) include learning that is holistic, reflexive, reflective, experiential and relational; recognising consequences of one's actions; and learning that involves patience and time. — [FNESC](https://www.fnesc.ca/first-peoples-principles-of-learning/); [SD61 teacher PDF](https://learn.sd61.bc.ca/wp-content/uploads/sites/96/2017/09/First-Peoples-Principles-of-Learning-for-Teachers.pdf); [FNESC poster](https://www.ecoforestry.ca/s/FNESC-Learning-First-Peoples-poster-11x17-hi-res-v2.pdf)

### Inferences
- [I] Several principles map directly onto the sim. "Recognizing the consequences of one's actions" corresponds to the meter reveals. "Patience and time" corresponds to multi-year delayed payoffs. "Relational" corresponds to partnership and consultation decisions. The "well-being of the land" maps onto a forest-health meter that is weighted equally with money.
- [I] Base Indigenous-partnership dilemmas on real, publicly documented BC community forests co-managed with or held by First Nations. Avoid assigning students Indigenous roles, which fits the brief's "play as yourselves" rule and avoids appropriation.
