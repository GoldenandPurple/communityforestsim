# ADST curriculum alignment

This doubles as the case for the teacher: it shows the simulation is not a novelty, it hits
named learning standards from the BC Applied Design, Skills, and Technologies (ADST) K-9
content the students already have. Strands below are drawn from the Entrepreneurship and
Marketing content (Grades 6-7, 8, and 9) and adjacent strands in the same document.

The pedagogical move throughout: students *use* each concept inside the simulation before it
is ever named. The debrief cashes them in afterward. Naming them first would kill the room.

## Direct hits

**Decision to seek profit or break even.**
The ADST 6-7 content lists pricing a product or service including the decision to seek profit
or break even. The whole treasury mechanic is this made physical: a community forest is not
trying to maximize profit, it is trying to stay viable enough to protect everything else. When
the treasury bleeds and the board has to decide how hard to cut, they are living this standard.

**Role of basic financial record-keeping / measurement of financial success and failure.**
6-7 lists basic financial record-keeping and budgeting; Grade 9 lists measurement of financial
success and failure. The four-meter scoreboard is a running measurement of exactly this, and the
insolvency end state is the failure case.

**Social entrepreneurship, including in First Nations communities.**
Grades 8 and 9 both name social entrepreneurship, and specifically its role in First Nations
communities. Round 3 (the co-management partnership) is this standard turned into a decision the
board makes. A community forest is itself a textbook case of social entrepreneurship: an
enterprise run for community benefit rather than private profit.

**Flow of goods and services from producers to consumers / who captures value.**
Grade 9 lists the flow of goods and services from producers to consumers. Round 2 (the outside
buyout) puts this in front of them as an ethical question: when an outside company buys the
rights, the value leaves the community. Community ownership is the counter-case.

**Market segmentation by demographic, geographic, psychographic, and purchasing pattern.**
Grade 9 names market segmentation. The three sentiment blocs (recreation and tourism, residents,
forestry) are stakeholder segmentation in action, and the impossibility of satisfying all three
at once is the lesson that segments have conflicting interests.

**Sources of financing for a new venture.**
Grade 9 lists sources of financing for a new venture or start-up. The forest's revenue-versus-
buyout-versus-partnership choices are a live version of where the money comes from and what each
source costs you in independence.

**Renewable versus non-renewable resources.**
The Metalwork strands (6-7, 8) explicitly frame metals as a non-renewable resource. A forest is
the renewable counterpart, but only if managed as one. The Forest Health slider makes the point
that a forest cut greedily becomes as gone as a mine: renewable in name only.

## Available as an optional exercise

**The 4 Ps (product, price, promotion, placement).**
Grade 9 names the 4 Ps directly. If the period allows, a short five-minute exercise applying the
4 Ps to the forest's timber or to its recreation/tourism value can be dropped in before Round 1.
Optional, because time is tight; the simulation stands without it.

**Recognition of a market need / identification of target market.**
Grade 8. Present in the framing of who the forest actually serves.

## Cross-curricular reach (bonus for the pitch)

Beyond ADST, the simulation naturally touches Social Studies (Indigenous rights and governance,
local government, resource economics) and Science (forest ecology, fire, watershed). If the
teacher wants to co-pitch it to a colleague or frame it for more than one course, that reach is
real.

## One-line version for the teacher

"It is a governance simulation where the class runs a community forest and has to keep it solvent
while balancing wildfire risk, three conflicting community groups, and an Indigenous
co-management decision. It hits profit-vs-break-even, social entrepreneurship including in First
Nations communities, renewable-vs-non-renewable, market segmentation, and value capture, and the
students use every concept before I name it."
